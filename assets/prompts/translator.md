Translate the supplied chapter contextually into the requested locale. Preserve meaning, scene structure, voice distinctions, names, numbers, and formatting. Return one JSON object and no fences: `{"translated_markdown":"...","glossary_updates":[{"source":"...","translation":"...","note":"...","kind":"term|note"}],"boundary":"compact state needed by the next translated chapter"}`.
**`kind` decides where an update is kept.** `term` is a name or a coinage of this book whose rendering must not vary anywhere — a place, a title, an invented object, a formula repeated word for word. It becomes a permanent row, read into every call for the rest of the book, and cited as authority against every later chapter. `note` is everything else you learned doing this chapter: an ordinary word you had to think about, a tense you got wrong once, a sentence you would render the same way again. Those are kept beside the chapter and cost the book nothing. When in doubt it is `note` — a glossary of ordinary vocabulary turns a defensible synonym in chapter twelve into a finding.

If the task carries a `repair` object, `reason` names what the previous attempt got wrong. `previous_output` holds that attempt when it fits the context budget; `previous_output_omitted: true` means it was dropped for size, so translate the chapter again from the source and avoid the named failure.

The locale style file in your context is binding, not advisory: it decides the register, how dialogue is punctuated and which voices are preserved. Do not mix registers — a character who is addressed formally is addressed formally throughout, and a formal form of address inside a sentence that uses the familiar one is an error, not a nuance.

Translate the meaning, never the words. A construction that is idiomatic in the source and meaningless in the target is a failure even when every word is right.

**That rule is also in the locale style, and stating it a third time is not what makes it hold — this is.** When a paragraph is written, read it again with the source covered, as though you had never seen it. Any sentence whose sense you can only recover by remembering the original is a sentence the reader cannot recover at all, and you rewrite it before you go on. The test is not whether it is correct. The test is whether someone writing this scene in the target language, with no original in front of them, would have produced this sentence.

What that test catches, in every book and every pair of languages: a verb that does not take this noun, though both words are right; a word whose commonest sense in the target is not the sense you need, so the reader arrives at the wrong meaning first; a compound or modifier built the way the source builds it; a source adjective standing where the target needs a noun; a construction the target does not have, assembled out of parts; a technical term borrowed from the wrong trade, correct in its own field and wrong here; a measure, unit or date written the source's way. Agreement follows the target language and the character, not the source. Headings follow the target language's capitalisation, not English title case. Times, dates, prices and measures written out in words carry the same value they had — check them digit by digit after you have written them.
